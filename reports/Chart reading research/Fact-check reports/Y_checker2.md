## 2. Notes per claim

### Y1 - Jiang, Kelly, Xiu (2023)
* Which version I read: the working paper, SSRN 3756587 (the NUS-hosted PDF, footer "Electronic copy available at ssrn.com/abstract=3756587"). The journal version (Dec 2023) was not readable: Wiley 403, SSRN 403. A vLex copy shows the published introduction, which leads with a weekly strategy: "gross Sharpe ratios as high as 7.2 for equal-weight portfolios and 1.7 for value-weight portfolios" and adds a fourth benchmark, TREND (Han, Zhou, Zhu 2016). So the published monthly numbers may differ from the ones checked here.
* Working-paper numbers, one-month hold, long-short (H-L) decile spread:
  * Equal-weight (Table 3): CNN 5-day images 2.35, 20-day 2.16, 60-day 1.29; momentum 0.25 (text rounds to 0.3), reversal 0.55, weekly reversal 1.23. Turnover 175%, 173%, 155% vs momentum 63%, reversal 168%, weekly reversal 167%.
  * Value-weight (Table 4): CNN 0.45, 0.49, 0.17; momentum 0.36; reversal 0.03; weekly reversal 0.30. Turnover 187%, 181%, 165% vs momentum 76%.
  * Text: "The monthly turnover of STR is 168% in our sample and MOM turnover is 63%. One-month holding period CNN strategies have essentially the same turnover as STR". Also "momentum ... typically viewed as a strategy that survives trading costs while STR does not."
* Rescaling and the linear model. Quote from the paper's introduction: "We find that a key component of image-based prediction accuracy is the implicit data scaling achieved by the image representation ... Following this data transformation, a linear model can produce a reasonable approximation to the CNN." Table 16 (20-day return horizon, 5-day images), Sharpe, equal / value weight: CNN 2.35 / 0.45; linear model on image-scaled numbers 2.07 / 0.66; linear model on plain return-scaled numbers 0.51 / 0.11. Text: "the linear pattern explains less than half of the content in CNN predictions" and the linear approximation is "generally inferior to the full non-linear CNN". This is the authors' own analysis. I found no independent critique that makes the rescaling point. What I did find: a GitHub replication (George-hardworking/reimaging-price-trends-replication-and-extension) whose US weekly equal-weight Sharpe is 5.85 vs the paper's 7.15 (I5/R5), and an SSRN paper (Guan, Li, Si, SSRN 6389479, page blocked, only a search summary seen) that says the open-high-low-close geometry drives the CNN's power. Neither is a "rescaling" critique.

### Y2
* STW: universe of 7,846 rules on the Dow (1897-1996). In the original 1897-1986 sample "certain trading rules did indeed outperform the benchmark, even after adjustment is made for data-snooping". Out of sample 1987-96: "The probability that the best-performing trading rule did not outperform the benchmark during this period is nearly 12 percent, suggesting that, at conventional levels of significance, there is scant evidence that technical trading rules were of any economic value". The best rule as of end-1986 in the full universe, a five-day moving average, earned "a mean return of 2.8 percent with a nominal p-value of 0.322 for the period 1987 to 1996". On the S&P 500 futures (1984-96) "we find no evidence that the trading rules outperform the benchmark". (Footnote 25: the best rule in the smaller 26-rule Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron set still returned 8.6% a year on the Dow in 1987-96; the paper's conclusion is that the best-performing rule "is not even statistically significant at standard critical levels".) They also list three possible reasons, including that markets became more efficient. So "failed" means "not statistically distinguishable from buy-and-hold", not "lost money".
* Marshall, Cahan, Cahan reuse the same 7,846-rule set on intraday data. I could only read the abstract (full text paywalled), so the sample details are unverified by me.
* Rink: 23 developed and 18 emerging markets, 6,406 rules, "up to 66 years", data end May 2016 (S&P 500 from Jan 1950). Because econstor and Springer showed a bot-check for me, I read the paper text in the research team's cached copy (research/txt/rink2023.txt); that is the paper itself, not their summary.

### Y3
* Definitions from the paper: liquidity demanders = trades above the quote midpoint (buys) or below (sells); midpoint trades discarded. Returns are 24-hour (buy at trade price, close at the bid 24 hours later). Sample: 100 randomly selected stocks per year, 2001-2006 (decimal era), more than 100 million transactions; scaled by (3,721 eligible firms / 100) over 6 years.
* Also in the paper: for conditional trades (buys when the ask falls to reach an integer, sells when the bid rises to it) the wealth transfer is positive, "$40 million per year", so the loss is specific to buying just below and selling just above round numbers.

### Y10 - 10-month average rule (exact timing)
1. Total market return each month = Mkt-RF + RF (percent, monthly file). Cash return = RF.
2. Build a total-return index P: start at 1 at the end of June 1926 and multiply by (1 + monthly return).
3. At the end of month t, compute the average of the last ten month-end index levels including month t (P_{t-9} ... P_t). Signal is ON if P_t is above that average.
4. The signal is applied to the next month only: month t+1 earns the market return if ON, RF if OFF. No look-ahead, no costs, no taxes.
5. First signal is available at the end of April 1927, so the first traded month is May 1927; the last is Aug 2026 (1,192 months). "98 calendar years" = the full years 1928-2025. Growth rates are geometric.

The standard deviation of one zero-skill annualised Sharpe from 504 daily returns is 0.710 (theory 0.707). R2 and R7 use the same formula (their table for N=28 says 1.45 and for N=243 says 2.0), so the two statements are consistent.
