# The Intelligent Investor (Benjamin Graham, with Jason Zweig commentary): study notes

Source read: `scratchpad/books/the_intelligent_investor.txt`, all ~30,240 lines. That covers the Buffett preface, Zweig's note on Graham, the Introduction, Chapters 1-20, the Postscript, every Zweig commentary, Appendixes 1-7 (including Buffett's "Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville"), the endnotes and the index.
All summaries are in my own words. Direct quotes are kept to single short sentences, 7 in total.
"G" means the point is in Graham's own text. "Z" means it comes from Zweig's commentary or footnotes.

## 1. What the book is about, and which edition

**Edition:** the Revised Edition (HarperBusiness, 2003). It reprints Graham's Fourth Revised Edition, which Graham updated in 1971-72 and which was first published in 1973. Jason Zweig added a commentary after every chapter and footnotes on Graham's pages, with data running through early 2003. Warren Buffett wrote the preface. The e-book metadata is from June 2003. In this edition "now" means early 1972 in Graham's text and early 2003 in Zweig's.

**What it is:** a practical book on investment *policy* and *temperament* for non-professionals, not a textbook on security analysis. It is organised around four ideas:
- **Investing is not speculating.** An investment has to be backed by analysis, protect your principal and aim for an adequate return.
- **Two investor types.**
  - *Defensive:* wants safety and little effort. Holds a bond/stock mix, diversified large-cap stocks bought at sensible prices, or index funds.
  - *Enterprising:* willing to do real work. Buys bargains, unpopular large companies and special situations, using strict quantitative screens.
- **Mr. Market.** Price swings are something you can use when they suit you, not a signal to follow.
- **Margin of safety.** Pay well below what you can show the business is worth, and diversify, so that errors and bad luck do not ruin you.

Zweig's commentary brings Graham's examples up to date with the 1999-2002 dot-com bubble and bust, and with the research on behavioural finance, fund costs and trading costs that has appeared since.

## 2. Key lessons (own words, with source)

1. **Price decides your return.** A great company bought at a silly price is a poor investment, and a mediocre company can be a sound one if it is cheap enough. (G, Intro, Ch. 8, Ch. 20; Z, Ch. 13 and 18 pair comparisons)
2. **The main risk is the investor's own behaviour, not the market.** Graham says the investor's chief problem "is likely to be himself." (G, Intro; Z, Ch. 8 and 20)
3. **Investment has a strict definition.** Graham's 1934 test: "An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis promises safety of principal and an adequate return." Anything else is speculation. Buying on margin or buying "hot" new issues counts as speculation by definition. (G, Ch. 1)
4. **Speculation is allowed, but only in a small, separate account.**
   - (G) Keep it in its own account, as small as possible. Never add money to it after gains, and never mix it with your investment thinking. (Ch. 1)
   - (Z) Put a hard ceiling of 10% of total wealth on this "mad money." (Ch. 1)
5. **Forecasting does not work, for the market or for industries.**
   - (G) Security prices are never predictable. Obvious growth in an industry, airlines being his example, does not turn into profits for investors. Graham grades his own past calls on the market level and admits they were less useful than simply following a consistent policy. (Intro, Ch. 1, Ch. 3)
   - (Z) Market-timing newsletters trailed an index fund. (Ch. 7)
6. **Published formulas decay once they become popular.** Graham shows this with the Dow Theory after 1938, formula-timing plans after 1950, his own "central value" method, and low-P/E Dow stocks in 1968-71. He writes that a method which "can be easily described and followed by a lot of people is by its terms too simple and too easy to last." (G, Ch. 1, 7, 8)
   - (Z) Adds the January effect, O'Shaughnessy's funds and the Motley Fool "Foolish Four" as data-mined failures. (Ch. 1)
7. **Timing and pricing are different things.** Buying because you forecast the next move (timing) makes you a speculator. Buying below value and selling well above it (pricing) is legitimate. Waiting for a signal only pays if it gets you a price low enough to make up for the income you lose while waiting. (G, Ch. 8)
8. **Mr. Market.** Your business partner offers each day to buy your share or sell you his, at a price driven by his mood. You can trade with him when his price is foolish and ignore him the rest of the time. An investor who panics at unjustified price drops turns his main advantage into a disadvantage. (G, Ch. 8; Z, Ch. 8)
9. **Expect large swings.** Over any five years, most holdings are likely to rise 50% or more from their low and fall a third or more from their high. The stock of a better company tends to be more speculative, because more of its price rests on the market's opinion rather than on its assets. (G, Ch. 8)
10. **Keep 25-75% in stocks and rebalance mechanically.** The formula restrains you as the market rises and gives you something rational to do as it falls. (G, Ch. 4 and 8; Z, Ch. 4)
11. **Growth stocks are hard to profit from even when the growth really comes.** Buyers have usually paid for the growth in advance, fast growth fades as a company gets bigger, and a slowdown in growth is punished harshly. (G, Ch. 5, 7 and 11; Appendix 4; Z, Ch. 7, 12 and 18)
    - (Z, Ch. 7) Very few large companies sustain 15-20% earnings growth for 10 years or more.
12. **The more precise the maths, the less reliable the valuation.** Growth-stock valuations built on higher mathematics mostly dress up speculation. (G, Ch. 11 and Appendix 4)
13. **Unpopular large companies and cheap secondary companies tend to be mispriced.** The market overreacts to temporary bad news and to neglect. (G, Ch. 7 and 15)
14. **Avoid new issues and anything that is being sold hard.** This covers IPOs, second-grade bonds and preferreds at full price, foreign bonds, and convertibles and warrants issued late in bull markets. (G, Ch. 6 and 16)
    - (Z, Ch. 6) The average IPO held for 3 years trailed the market by about 23 percentage points a year (1980-2001).
15. **Costs and turnover eat returns.** (Z, Ch. 6 and 9)
    - Barber and Odean: the most active traders lagged the market by about 6.4 points a year after costs.
    - The typical active fund needed to beat the market by about 3.5 points before costs just to match it.
    - Only about 15% of funds beat the Vanguard 500 index fund over 20 years.
    - Fund investors lagged their own funds by about 4.7 points a year by buying high and selling low (1998-2001).
16. **Look behind the reported earnings.** Watch for "special charges" that keep recurring, big-bath write-offs, pro forma figures, capitalised costs, dilution from options and warrants, and pension-income assumptions. Use multi-year average earnings, never forward estimates. (G, Ch. 12 and 17; Z, Ch. 12 and 17)
17. **Margin of safety is the single idea behind the book.** Graham boils sound investing down to three words, "MARGIN OF SAFETY." (G, Ch. 20)
    - For bonds, it is how many times earnings cover interest.
    - For typical stocks, it is how far the earnings yield exceeds the bond yield. Over about 10 years the gap should add up to roughly 50% of the price paid.
    - For bargains, it is the gap between appraised value and price.
    - Diversification is its natural partner.
18. **Consequences matter more than probabilities.** Size positions so that being wrong is survivable. Zweig uses Pascal's wager and Kahneman's "well-calibrated confidence / correctly-anticipated regret." (Z, Ch. 20 and Postscript)
19. **Adequate results are easy and superior results are hard.** Graham: "To achieve satisfactory investment results is easier than most people realize; to achieve superior results is harder than it looks." (G, Ch. 20)
    - (G) Trying to add a little cleverness often makes results worse. (Intro)
20. **Practise before using real money.** (Z, Ch. 15, citing a 1976 Graham interview) Spend about a year picking stocks on paper, then compare the results with an S&P 500 index fund. Only if you enjoyed it and did well should you commit real money, and then at most 10% of the portfolio. (Z, Ch. 14 and 15) The rest belongs in an index fund.

## 3. Codeable rules (thresholds exactly as in the book)

### 3.1 Stock/bond allocation and rebalancing (G, Ch. 1 and 4; Z, Ch. 4)
- **Bands:** stocks never below 25% or above 75% of the fund, with bonds taking the rest.
- **Default split:** 50/50.
- **Rebalancing trigger:** act when a drift of about 5 points puts stocks at 55% or 45%. At 55%, sell 1/11 of the stocks and put the money in bonds. At 45%, use 1/11 of the bonds to buy stocks.
- **Optional tilt:** move stocks toward 25% when the market looks dangerously high and toward 75% after a long bear market. Graham warns he can give no reliable rule for this. In early 1972 he advised no more than 50% in stocks.
- **"Cold-blooded" variant:** hold 25% stocks until the Dow's dividend yield reaches about 2/3 of the high-grade bond yield, then return to 50/50.
- **Zweig's version:** set the target from your life circumstances, not your age, and change it only when your life changes. Rebalance on a fixed schedule every 6 months (for example 1 January and 4 July). Go to 100% stocks only if you pass all of these:
  - at least 1 year of living expenses in cash;
  - 20 or more years of steady investing ahead;
  - you held through the 2000 bear market and bought more during it;
  - you have a written behaviour plan.
- **1964/1972 caution list, in order of urgency:**
  1. No borrowing to buy or hold securities.
  2. No increase in the share held in stocks.
  3. Cut stocks back to a maximum of 50% if needed.
- **Bond maturity:**
  - (G, Ch. 8) High-grade bonds maturing in 7 years or less barely move with rates.
  - (Z, Ch. 4) The simple default is intermediate bonds of 5-10 years.

### 3.2 Dollar-cost averaging (G, Intro, Ch. 1, 3 and 5; Z, Ch. 5 and 8)
- Invest the same dollar amount every month or quarter, whatever the level of the market.
- Evidence Graham cites (Tomlinson): 23 ten-year DCA periods in the Dow stocks ending 1929-1952 all showed a profit either at the end of the buying period or within 5 years. The average profit was 21.5%, not counting dividends.
- Graham's caution: do not *start* a new DCA plan at a clearly high market level, because most people will not stick with it through early losses.
- (Z) The ideal is automatic DCA into index funds, for example $300 total US / $100 international / $100 bonds each month. Sign the "Investment Owner's Contract," a commitment to hold for at least 10 years.

### 3.3 Defensive investor, first version (G, Ch. 5)
- **Diversification:** at least 10 and at most about 30 stocks.
- **Large, prominent and conservatively financed:**
  - *Conservative financing:* common equity at book value is at least 50% of total capitalisation including bank debt. For railroads and utilities, at least 30%.
  - *Large:* at least $50M of assets or $50M of sales (1972 dollars).
    - (Z, Ch. 5) Today's equivalent is a market cap of at least $10B.
    - (Z, Ch. 14) At least $2B.
  - *Prominent:* in the top quarter or top third of its industry by size.
- **Dividends:** paid continuously since at least 1950, i.e. about 20 years.
  - (Z) At least 10 years today, and 20 years is not too restrictive.
- **Price:** at most 25× average earnings of the past 7 years and at most 20× trailing-12-month earnings. Graham notes this rules out nearly all popular growth stocks.
- **Growth stock definition** (G, Ch. 5): earnings per share (EPS) expected at least to double in 10 years, i.e. above 7.1% a year.
- **Portfolio review:** at most once a year, with few changes.
- (Z) If you would trade more than twice a year or spend more than 1-2 hours a month, use index funds instead.

### 3.4 Defensive investor, the seven statistical tests (G, Ch. 13-14)
1. **Adequate size.** Industrials: annual sales of at least $100M. Utilities: total assets of at least $50M (1972 dollars).
2. **Strong financial condition.**
   - Industrials: current assets at least 2× current liabilities, *and* long-term debt no greater than net current assets (working capital).
   - Utilities: debt no more than 2× stock equity at book value. The current-ratio test does not apply.
3. **Earnings stability.** Some earnings for the common stock in each of the past 10 years (no deficit year).
4. **Dividend record.** Uninterrupted payments for at least the past 20 years.
5. **Earnings growth.** EPS up at least one-third over 10 years, comparing the 3-year average at the start with the 3-year average at the end.
   - (Z) This is below 3% a year. Also try 50% (about 4% a year) or 100% (about 7% a year).
6. **Moderate P/E.** Price no more than 15× average earnings of the last 3 years.
7. **Moderate price to assets.** Price no more than 1.5× last reported book value.
   - Exception: a P/E below 15 can justify a higher price/book, as long as **P/E × P/B ≤ 22.5**. For example, 9× earnings with 2.5× book passes. (This implies the popular "Graham number" √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS), which is a derivation, not a formula in the book.)

Portfolio-level rules that go with the seven tests:
- **Earnings yield vs bonds:** the whole stock portfolio's earnings yield (E/P) should be at least the current AA bond yield. In 1972 that meant P/E ≤ 13.3 against a 7.5% bond yield.
  - (Z) With a 4.6% AA yield in 2003, the maximum P/E is 21.7, and a typical holding should be about 20% below that, i.e. about 17× 3-year average earnings.
- **Portfolio P/E:** the 15× cap should give an average multiple of about 12-13.
- **Financial companies:** apply the same P/E and P/B arithmetic to banks and insurers.
- **Railroads:** own them only when the value on offer is overwhelming.
- **Old-style alternative:** buy equal amounts of all 30 Dow stocks (today, a total-market index fund).
- (G, Ch. 8) Stay within about **1/3 above tangible book value**, i.e. P/TBV ≤ 1.33. Also require a satisfactory earnings/price ratio, a strong financial position and earnings that should at least hold up.

### 3.5 Enterprising investor
**Low-multiplier screen** (G, Ch. 15, applied to the Stock Guide at the end of 1970):
- Starting filter: P/E of 9 or less on trailing-12-month earnings.
- Financial condition: current assets at least 1.5× current liabilities, and debt no more than 110% of net current assets (industrials).
- Earnings stability: no deficit in the last 5 years.
- Dividend record: some current dividend.
- Earnings growth: last year's earnings above those of about 4-5 years earlier (1966 in the example).
- Price: below 120% of net tangible assets.
- No minimum size, provided the stocks are bought carefully and as a group.
- Optional seventh filter: an S&P earnings-and-dividend rank of B+ (average) or better. Graham's rough count was about 150 stocks passing out of 4,500, or about 100 with the rank filter.
- (G) Be less rigid than the defensive investor: let a clear strength offset a small weakness. For example, one deficit year like 1970 can be accepted if average earnings are large.

**Bargain definition** (G, Ch. 7 and 20):
- An issue is a true bargain only if its indicated value is at least 50% above the price, i.e. price ≤ 2/3 of value.
- Secondary (non-leading) companies should be bought only at 2/3 or less of appraised value. Graham also states a preference for asset values of at least 2/3 of the market price (Ch. 15).

**Unpopular large company** (G, Ch. 7):
- Buy the 6 or 10 lowest-P/E Dow stocks every year and hold for 1-5 years.
- Graham cites a Drexel study, 1937-69:
  - low-multiplier stocks beat the Dow clearly in 25 of 34 years, tied in 6 and lost clearly in 3;
  - $10,000 grew to $66,900, against $44,000 for all 30 Dow stocks and $25,300 for the high-P/E group.
  - The method failed in 1917-33, and a 1968-to-mid-1971 test lost money.
- Graham's added safeguards:
  - price low relative to *past average earnings*, not just current earnings (this avoids cyclicals at peak earnings);
  - at least 10 years without a deficit;
  - large size and financial strength;
  - price below both the past average price and the past average P/E.

**Net-current-asset ("net-net") stocks** (G, Ch. 1, 7 and 15):
- Net current asset value (NCAV) = current assets minus *all* liabilities and preferred stock, giving zero value to plant and every other asset.
- Graham-Newman bought at **2/3 or less of NCAV**, usually holding **at least 100 issues**.
- Results cited:
  - 85 stocks bought at the end of 1957 and held 2 years gained 75%, against 50% for the S&P 425. Only 7 were flat and none had significant losses.
  - Burton-Dixie: bought at 20 against NCAV of 30, bought out at 53¾ after 3½ years.
- Practical filter for 1971: drop net-nets that reported a loss in the last 12 months.
- Graham-Newman dropped two categories after about 10 years of records, because results were not dependable enough:
  - "attractive" stocks that could not be bought below working capital;
  - "unrelated" hedges.

**Special situations** (G, Ch. 15): take arbitrages and liquidations only where the calculated annual return is at least 20% *and* the estimated chance of success is at least 4 in 5.

**Growth-stock limit for enterprising investors** (G, Ch. 7): avoid growth stocks at a P/E above about 20. Graham says this is roughly the same test as 25× the 7-year average.

**Bonds and preferreds for enterprising investors** (G, Ch. 6):
- Buy second-grade issues only at bargain levels: at least 30% below par for high-coupon issues, and much lower for low coupons. A second-grade 5.5-6% bond at par is almost always a bad buy.
- Minimum interest coverage for investment-grade bonds (G, Ch. 11):

| Type | Before tax, 7-yr average | Before tax, poorest year | After tax, 7-yr average | After tax, poorest year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Utility | 4.0× | 3.0× | 2.65× | 2.10× |
| Railroad | 5.0× | 4.0× | 3.20× | 2.65× |
| Industrial | 7.0× | 5.0× | 4.30× | 3.20× |
| Retail | 5.0× | 4.0× | 3.20× | 2.65× |

- For preferreds, use fixed charges plus 2× the preferred dividend.
- Alternative test for high-rate periods: pre-tax earnings as a percentage of debt principal of at least 33% (industrials), 25% (railroads) or 20% (utilities). The poorest-year requirement is about 2/3 of the 7-year requirement.

**Convertibles** (G, Ch. 16):
- "Never convert a convertible."
- A fixed policy of selling at 25-30% profit caps gains, while losses stay uncapped.
- Adjust per-share figures for warrants by adding the warrants' market value to the share price. Graham's calculation for National General took the "true" price from $24.50 to $43.30.

### 3.6 Valuation formula (G, Ch. 11)
- **Growth-stock value = current (normal) earnings × (8.5 + 2g)**, where g is the expected annual growth rate over the next 7-10 years. Graham's table:

| g | 0% | 2.5% | 5% | 7.2% | 10% | 14.3% | 20% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiple | 8.5 | 13.5 | 18.5 | 22.9 | 28.5 | 37.1 | 48.5 |

- Graham says this only approximates the more elaborate calculations and is not "true value." If growth above about 8% is assumed forever, the value becomes infinite, so a margin of safety is essential.
- **Growth-rate measurement** (G, Ch. 12): compare the 3-year average EPS with the 3-year average 10 years earlier. Include special charges in the averages.
- **Two-part appraisal** (G, Ch. 11): first compute a mechanical "past-performance value" from profitability, stability, growth and financial condition. Then adjust it explicitly for expected changes, and record both figures.

### 3.7 Zweig's modern quantitative flags (Z, Ch. 5, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15 and 19)
- **Fund expense caps:**
  - taxable or muni bonds: 0.75%;
  - US large/mid equity: 1.0%;
  - high-yield bonds: 1.0%;
  - US small-cap: 1.25%;
  - foreign: 1.50%;
  - convertible funds: 1.0%;
  - emerging-market bond funds: 1.25%.
- **Fund selection order:** expenses first, then risk (worst quarterly loss, then Morningstar), then past performance last. Avoid persistent losers with high fees.
- **Balance sheet:** long-term debt below 50% of total capital.
- **Owner earnings:** grown at least 6-7% a year on average over 10 years.
- **ROIC** (owner earnings / invested capital): at least 10% is attractive; 6-7% can be acceptable with strong brands.
- **Acquisitions:** more than 2-3 a year ("serial acquirer") is a warning sign.
- **Institutional ownership:** above 60% means the stock is "over-owned."
- **Stock options:** vote against grants above 3% of shares outstanding.
- **Pensions:** an assumed return on plan assets above 6.5% (as of 2003) is implausible. Pension income above 5% of net income is a warning sign.
- **Cash flows:** operating cash flow persistently negative while financing cash flow is persistently positive marks an "OPM addict" (Other People's Money).
- **Gold:** at most 2% of assets through a precious-metals fund (5% if over 65).
- **TIPS:** at least 10% of retirement assets.
- **Emerging-market bonds:** at most 10% of the bond portfolio.
- **Foreign stocks:** up to 1/3 of stock money.
- **Behaviour:** do not buy after a stock or market has gone up and do not sell after it has gone down. Graham: "Never buy a stock immediately after a substantial rise or sell one immediately after a substantial drop."

## 4. Psychology principles

- **Mr. Market (G, Ch. 8).** Market prices are an offer, not an instruction. Use them when they are silly and ignore them otherwise. The listed investor's advantage over a private owner is that he has the *option* to act on quotes. Letting quotes drive his feelings throws that advantage away. Zweig adds (Ch. 8):
  - The brain sees trends in random sequences (dopamine after two or three repeats).
  - Losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains.
  - Investors who received frequent news earned about half the returns of those who received none (Andreassen).
- **Margin of safety (G, Ch. 20).** The buffer that makes a precise forecast unnecessary.
  - It depends on the price paid: large at one price, gone at a higher one.
  - It works statistically across a diversified group, like an insurer's underwriting margin or a roulette wheel that pays 39:1 instead of 35:1.
  - A low enough price can turn a mediocre security into a sound investment, for example real-estate bonds after a 90% fall.
  - Buffett's bridge analogy (Appendix 1): build for 30,000 lb, drive 10,000-lb trucks.
- **Investor vs speculator (G, Ch. 1 and 8; Appendix 4).**
  - The investor judges price by standards of value; the speculator judges value by price.
  - The investor's main interest is buying and holding suitable securities at suitable prices. The speculator's main interest is anticipating and profiting from price moves.
  - Graham's 1958 lecture (Appendix 4): paying heavily for expected future growth brings speculation into even the best companies. The risk curve is U-shaped, with the lowest speculative element in the middle ground of sound, moderately priced companies.
- **Temperament beats IQ (G, Intro; Z, Intro).** Newton lost money in the South Sea bubble. LTCM was run by Nobel laureates. Buffett: the book gives the framework, and the reader has to supply the emotional discipline.
- **Rule of opposites (G, Ch. 3; Z, Ch. 3).** Public enthusiasm is highest near tops (1929, 1968, 1999) and lowest near bottoms (1949, 1974, 2002). Graham's warning signs of a bull-market top (G, Ch. 8):
  - historically high price level;
  - high P/E ratios;
  - low dividend yields relative to bond yields;
  - heavy margin buying;
  - a flood of low-quality new issues.
- **No middle ground in investor type (G, Ch. 7).** Choose defensive or enterprising. Being "half a businessman" usually disappoints. Graham does allow a middle ground in *securities*.
- **Controllables (Z, Ch. 8).** Costs, expenses, expectations, risk (allocation, diversification, rebalancing), taxes, and above all your own behaviour.
- **Well-calibrated confidence and correctly anticipated regret (Z, Ch. 20).** Before any bet, ask:
  - What is my track record on decisions like this?
  - Who is on the other side, and what do they know?
  - What do I lose if I am wrong, and how will I react?

## 5. Claims to check against modern evidence

These points come from my own general knowledge and were **not verified** in this session. Treat them as leads to check, not as facts.

| Claim (source) | What later evidence suggests | Verdict for us |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap stocks (low P/E, low P/B) beat expensive ones (G, Ch. 7, 15 and 18; Z, Ch. 18 cites Fama and French) | Strong premium in US data roughly 1926-2006 (Fama-French HML). US value then lagged growth badly from about 2007 to 2020, one of its longest drawdowns. It rebounded sharply in 2021-22 and was weak again afterwards. Book value is a worse yardstick now that intangibles (R&D, brands) are expensed. Zweig himself notes (Ch. 14) that intangibles push up P/B. Post-publication decay applies (McLean-Pontiff, already in our playbook). | Premium is real but episodic and can underperform for a decade. Earnings- or cash-flow-based value holds up better than P/B alone. |
| Net-nets gave dependable excess returns 1923-57 (G, Ch. 7 and 15) | Oppenheimer (1986) found very high returns for NCAV stocks from 1970-83. Later international studies also found excess returns, but concentrated in illiquid micro-caps, often in Japan or other non-US markets. In the US today, true net-nets are rare, tiny, often loss-making, sometimes frauds or shells. Graham himself said the opportunities disappeared after 1957 and only returned in 1970. | Probably still an anomaly, but not tradable for a small bot on a megacap/ETF universe. |
| Low-P/E Dow stocks ("Dogs of the Dow") beat the Dow (G, Ch. 7; Z, Ch. 1 and 7) | After it became popular in the 1990s, results were roughly in line with the Dow, with long losing stretches. Graham's own 1968-71 test lost money. | Weak edge once costs and publicity are counted. |
| Graham's 7 defensive tests pick sound, safe stocks (G, Ch. 14) | Screens like this (for example AAII's "Graham Defensive") show mixed results, and in most years very few US stocks pass all 7, mainly because of P/B ≤ 1.5 and a 2:1 current ratio. Zweig counted how many S&P 500 stocks passed each test in 2003 (about 120 for the current ratio, 185 for P/E, 123 for P/B, 142 for P/E × P/B). | Useful as a *quality/avoid-junk filter*, not as a source of alpha. |
| Market valuation predicts long-run returns (G, Ch. 3; Z, Ch. 3 on Shiller CAPE above 20 leading to poor returns; Z's 2003 estimate of about 6% a year) | CAPE has had real power for 10-year expected returns historically, but almost none as a timing tool: US CAPE stayed above 20 for most of 1990-2024 while returns were often strong. Zweig's 2003 estimate of about 6% nominal turned out too low for 2003-2024. | Use only to set *expectations*, never to time the market. Graham himself says his level calls were less useful than steady policy. |
| DCA is the most reliable formula (G, Ch. 5, citing Tomlinson) | A Vanguard 2012 study found lump-sum investing beat 12-month DCA about 2/3 of the time, because markets tend to rise. DCA's value is behavioural: it reduces regret and panic. | Not relevant for a fully funded bot. |
| "Technical approaches" (buying because prices rose) never work lastingly (G, Intro; Ch. 8 on the Dow Theory) | Contradicted by the momentum literature: cross-sectional momentum (Jegadeesh-Titman 1993) and time-series trend-following across asset classes (Moskowitz-Ooi-Pedersen; Faber's 10-month SMA) persisted out of sample. They carry crash risk and weakened after publication. Graham's point that popular formulas decay still applies. | Graham is too absolute here. But his warning explains why we halve backtests and keep sleeve C on probation. |
| Active funds lag the index by roughly their costs (G, Ch. 9 and 15; Z, Ch. 9) | Strongly confirmed by SPIVA reports: over 15-20 years, roughly 85-90%+ of US active funds lag their benchmark. | Robust. |
| Heavy trading destroys returns (Z, Ch. 1 and 6: Barber-Odean) | Confirmed repeatedly, including in the Brazilian day-trader data our playbook already cites. | Robust, and it matches our turnover limits. |
| Dividend payers and dividend increases predict better future earnings (Z, Ch. 19: Arnott-Asness 2003; Nissim-Ziv) | Arnott-Asness is contested. Buybacks have largely replaced dividends since then, and "dividend" factor returns overlap with value and quality. | Weak as a standalone signal. |

## 6. Fit with the bot (critical)

**What the bot is:** daily, long-only, ETF-heavy, with four sleeves:
- A: Faber GTAA-5 trend, 50-60%;
- B: RSI(2) dips, 15-25%;
- C: Minervini breakouts on 40 megacaps, on probation, 10-20%;
- D: crypto trend, optional, 0-10%.

It has **no fundamentals data**.

**The core tension:** most of the bot is exactly what Graham calls speculation. Sleeves A, C and D buy *because prices went up*, the "technical approach" he dismisses in the Introduction and Chapter 8. Sleeve C buys high-momentum growth leaders near 52-week highs, which is his clearest warning case. His "never buy right after a substantial rise" contradicts breakout buying head-on. Zweig (Ch. 10) even lists "technical analysis" and "market timing" as reasons to reject an adviser.

We should not pretend the bot is a Graham portfolio. The honest framing: in Graham's terms the bot is an *enterprising speculative* operation. His advice for such operations is:
- limit the money at stake;
- keep it separate;
- judge it on long records;
- expect published formulas to decay.

Our playbook already does these things. The useful parts of Graham for us are his *risk and behaviour* rules, not his stock screens.

**Bottom line for the bot:** Graham's screens need fundamentals we do not have and mostly do not suit a daily momentum/trend system. His *policy* rules transfer well, and the bot already follows most of them:
- keep speculation small and separate;
- cap equity near 75%;
- rebalance mechanically;
- keep costs and turnover low;
- set expectations at 6-10%;
- fix behaviour rules in advance;
- practise on paper for a year and benchmark against simple alternatives.

The concrete additions worth making:
1. a combined C+D cap;
2. a 75% equity cap;
3. ETF expense caps;
4. a formal paper-to-live gate benchmarked against 60/40;
5. later, a fundamentals-based *quality* filter (not a value filter) on sleeve C's universe.
